# Structure in long-form fiction

Structure is not an outline. It is a ledger of promises and payoffs, and the
reader's experience of it is the book keeping its word.

## The scene is the unit

A scene earns its place by changing something. Before writing one, be able to
say:

- **What the POV character wants right now** — in this scene, not in the story.
  Concretely, and soon. A scene where nobody wants anything is a summary wearing
  a scene's clothes.
- **What is in the way** — a person, a rule, their own fear, a deadline. Pressure
  that cannot fail is not pressure.
- **What is different at the end** — a decision made, an ally lost, information
  gained, a door closed. "Nothing, but it was atmospheric" means it belongs
  inside another scene, or it is a short story of its own.

If the scene ends in the same posture it started, it is a beat you haven't
written yet.

## Scene and aftermath

Alternating pressure with reflection is what keeps a long book readable. After a
scene that cost the character something, they need a beat to absorb it — but
write that beat with a decision in it, or cut it. Reflection that ends in the
same place it started is the most common reason a middle sags.

## Point of view is a promise

- One POV per scene. Switching mid-scene, even once, teaches the reader that the
  camera is a liar, and they stop trusting anything the narration says.
- The reader can know only what the POV knows. That constraint is not a
  limitation, it is the engine: **tension is information asymmetry**. Let the
  reader know what the character does not, or let the character know and be
  unable to act, and a quiet scene becomes unbearable.
- What the POV notices characterises them. A locksmith counts locks; a liar
  watches hands. Detail chosen by the POV is free characterisation; detail chosen
  by the author is research showing through.

## The promise ledger

Keep a list, in the file, of every question the book opens and where it closes:
the object introduced in chapter two, the secret mentioned once, the debt, the
injured animal, the unanswered letter. A book is a set of contracts.

- **Every promise gets paid, or is deliberately left open** and the reader knows
  it was deliberate. An accident left dangling reads as an author who forgot.
- **Payoff should be earned, not just delivered.** The reader should be able to
  look back and see the groundwork — the gun on the wall is not a surprise, the
  knowing of it is.
- **Closing the ledger is the last act.** Count the open threads before the
  climax; whatever is still open needs either a scene or an explicit decision to
  leave it.

## Endings

- **Chapter endings** turn on a change or a question. Never on a summary of what
  the chapter just did — the reader was there.
- **Scene endings** can land on a line of dialogue, an image, or an action.
  Falling action belongs at the start of the next scene, not the end of this one.
- **The final chapter** answers the question the book actually posed, which is
  often not the plot question. If the plot is solved but the character is
  unchanged, the ending will feel hollow and no amount of epilogue fixes it.

## Diagnosing a sagging middle

Do not add events. Check three things in order:

1. **Does the character want something they can lose this chapter?** A middle
   sags when the want goes generic — "find the truth" instead of "get into room
   4 before the shift change".
2. **Has any scene ended without a cost?** Free progress drains tension. Make
   something be given up, even something small.
3. **Are the subplots alternating or waiting?** Threads that pause for chapters
   read as abandoned. Interleave, and keep each one's last beat recent enough to
   remember.

## Revision order

Structure before scene, scene before line. Editing sentences you are about to
cut is the most expensive mistake in revision, and it feels exactly like work.

1. Pass one, structure only: the ledger, what turns, what is missing, what
   repeats. Cut scenes here. Be ruthless; it is cheaper now than at any later
   point.
2. Pass two, scene: wants, obstacles, outcomes, POV discipline.
3. Pass three, line: rhythm, diction, and only now the sentences.

Read the draft aloud at pass three. Anything you stumble over is a sentence
problem, and anything you skim aloud is a scene problem that survived pass two.
