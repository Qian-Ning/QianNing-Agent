# Dialogue that carries weight

Dialogue is not the place characters say things. It is the place they **do**
things to each other, using words instead of hands.

## Every line is a move

Before writing a line, know what the speaker is trying to accomplish with it:
persuade, deflect, test, wound, placate, buy time, hide something, force a
reaction.

- If a line accomplishes nothing, cut it. Chatter that only fills space makes the
  real lines invisible.
- If two characters want the same thing and say so, the scene is over before it
  starts. Conflict comes from opposed wants in the same room.
- The most interesting dialogue is where a character wants something and cannot
  ask for it directly.

## People do not answer the question they were asked

Real conversation is full of side-stepping, and writing that down is what makes
dialogue feel alive:

- Answering a different question — often the one they wish had been asked.
- Answering the subtext instead of the words.
- Deflecting with a question, a joke, or a complaint about something trivial.
- Saying something true but irrelevant, because the relevant thing costs too
  much.
- Silence, which is a line like any other and often the strongest one.

## Subtext: what is meant versus what is said

Underneath each line there is a want. The reader is engaged when they can feel
the want and the words do not match.

- Two people discussing dinner while negotiating whether one of them is leaving.
- A character stating a plain fact that is obviously a plea.
- The gap is the scene. If the words and the want are the same thing, you are
  writing a memo.

## Never let them explain what the other one knows

- "As you know, our father died in the fire" is the classic failure — nobody says
  what the listener already knows unless the saying itself is the move (taunting,
  reminding, threatening).
- Exposition belongs in the friction: a stranger who does not know, a record
  someone reads, an argument where one person gets a detail wrong.
- If a character must explain something for the reader's benefit, make explaining
  cost them something, or make the other person refuse to hear it.

## Beats: let the body contradict the mouth

The physical action between lines is what tells the reader what the words are
doing.

- A beat of action instead of a speech tag: *She didn't look up.* "Fine."
- The body says what the character will not: the glass put down too hard, the
  hand going to the door, the laugh a half-second late.
- Beats also control rhythm. A pause before an answer changes the answer's
  meaning entirely.
- Use them to break up long speeches; nobody talks for a paragraph without doing
  anything.

## Sound like speech, not like writing

- **Interrupt.** People cut each other off, and the interruption is a plot event.
- **Trail off.** An unfinished sentence carries more than a finished one.
- **Overlap and misunderstand.** Answer the accusation they think they heard,
  not the one you made.
- **Contradict.** A character can start a sentence and reverse it mid-course.
- **Drop the greetings.** Start late, in the middle of the argument. Entering a
  room and exchanging pleasantries is the first thing to cut.
- **Use names sparingly.** Real people use each other's names rarely — mostly to
  start, to make a point, or when angry.
- **Fragment freely.** Speech is full of half-sentences and dropped subjects.
  Written grammar in dialogue reads as a lecture.

## Accents and dialect: hint, do not transcribe

- Transcribing an accent phonetically is exhausting to read and reads as mockery.
  Two or three markers, chosen for rhythm, are enough to place the voice.
- Word order, idiom, and what a character notices do far more work than dropped
  letters.
- If the reader cannot parse a line in one pass, they lose the scene and stop
  hearing the character.

## The test: could only this person say it

Take a line out of context. If any character in the book could have said it, it
is not doing characterisation work.

- Vocabulary, rhythm, and what they avoid — a person who never finishes a
  sentence, one who uses three words where one would do, one who answers in
  questions.
- Give major characters a consistent way of handling conflict (a deflector, a
  escalator, a quiet one who lands the hardest blow), and let it be visible in
  how they speak, not in a description of their manner.
- Never have a character speak a theme aloud. The reader should build the
  meaning; the characters should be busy trying to get what they want.

## Tags

- `said` is invisible. Use it.
- Avoid decorated tags — *exclaimed*, *hissed*, *growled* — used for ordinary
  lines. They draw attention to the writing.
- Do not attach an adverb to every tag. If `he said softly` is doing work, the
  line was not written softly enough.
- An action beat can replace a tag entirely, which is usually better: it keeps
  the reader in the room instead of in the narration.

## Revision pass

Read the scene aloud, both parts, playing it. Wherever you stumble, the line is
not speakable. Wherever you skim, the scene is not doing anything. Both are
fixable, and both are invisible on the page.
