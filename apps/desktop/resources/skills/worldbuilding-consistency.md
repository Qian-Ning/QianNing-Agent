# Keeping a world consistent

Consistency is not a virtue in itself. It is what lets a reader stop checking and
start believing, and it is what stops a later chapter from quietly contradicting
an earlier one in a way that makes the whole book feel unplanned.

## Separate hard rules from soft rules

The most useful thing you can do for a long work is decide, explicitly, which
facts **cannot** change and which are yours to bend.

- **Hard rules**: the ones the plot depends on, the magic or technology's
  limits, the geography that travel times hang on, the timeline. Breaking one is
  a retcon, and readers notice.
- **Soft rules**: texture, local custom, dialect, what a minor character
  believes. These can drift, and drift in these often reads as life.

A rule that is inconsistent in **application** but consistent in **limit** is
usually fine. A rule that changes its limit is usually a problem.

## Every power needs a cost

Unlimited capability removes tension, and a reader who notices stops worrying
about the characters.

- What does using it take — time, material, health, memory, a promise?
- What can it *not* do, and does the plot respect that limit even when it would
  be convenient not to?
- Who has access, and what does that concentration mean politically? Power
  distributed is a different world from power hoarded.

Write the cost down next to the capability. The cost is usually where the best
scenes come from, because the limit is what forces a character to choose.

## Names

- Keep the sound system consistent: if the northern names are clipped and
  consonant-heavy, do not drop a flowing vowel-heavy name in the same village
  without a reason the reader will accept.
- **Choose one spelling** and put it in the bible. Inconsistent diacritics,
  transliteration, and hyphenation are the most common small errors and the most
  jarring, because they look like carelessness rather than choice.
- Track titles, honorifics, and forms of address. How a character is addressed
  changes with who is speaking, and that is characterisation, not trivia.
- Avoid names that look or sound alike for characters who share scenes. The
  reader is reading quickly, at night, possibly tired.

## Timeline arithmetic

Do this on paper, not in your head:

- Travel times between the places the plot actually uses, in the seasons it uses
  them. Weather and roads change, and a journey that took four days in autumn
  taking two in winter needs an explanation.
- Ages, and how they interact with the calendar and with each other.
- The order of events across subplots: when two threads are running at once,
  mark where each one's beats fall relative to the other.
- Anything that recurs — a festival, a harvest, a term of service — must land
  where the calendar says it lands.

Most continuity breaks are arithmetic, not imagination.

## Who knows what, and when

The most common logic break in long fiction is a character acting on information
they have not been given, or failing to act on information they have.

- Keep a per-character ledger of what they know, when they learned it, and
  whether it is **true** or merely believed.
- Distinguish what they know from what they merely suspect, and write suspicion
  down; it is what drives a scene where a character does something apparently
  irrational.
- A reveal is only a reveal if the reader can trace the path by which the
  information travelled.

## Consequences compound

Once something is established, it is true forever, and the world has to keep
behaving as if it were.

- If a city burned, trade routes shift, prices change, and refugees exist in the
  next chapter.
- If a character's secret came out, everyone who cares about it behaves
  differently afterwards — including people who were not present.
- Run the check before a new chapter: what happened earlier that must still be
  visible here?

## The author must know the truth

Characters may be wrong, records may lie, and tales may be exaggerated. That is
depth, not inconsistency — as long as **you** know which way round it is, and the
text eventually lets the reader in on it.

The failure case is the author being confused along with the character. Keep a
separate column: what is true, and what is believed.

## Retcon discipline

When new material contradicts something already written, prefer changing the
**cause** to changing the **consequences**.

- Adjust what caused the old fact to be true, and the old scene remains intact.
- Changing the consequence means every later scene that depended on it is now
  wrong, and the reader will go looking.

If a genuine contradiction has to stand, decide whether to lampshade it in-world
(a mistake, a lie, an unreliable narrator) or accept it and stop pretending the
world is tidy. Readers forgive either; they do not forgive silence.

## Keeping it usable

- **One source of truth**, and one only: a bible, a notes file, a spreadsheet.
  Two places that can disagree will.
- **Update it in the same sitting** as the drafted scene, not later. "I will add
  it to the notes later" is how continuity drifts.
- **Check before writing, not after**: the three things a scene is most likely to
  break are the timeline, a character's knowledge, and a rule's limit.
