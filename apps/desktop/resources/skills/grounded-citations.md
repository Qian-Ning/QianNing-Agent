An answer that cannot be checked is an opinion. Cite as you go, from sources you
actually opened — the point of a citation is that the reader can follow it to
the same conclusion.

## Open the source before you cite it

- A search result, a snippet, a title, or a model's memory of a page is not a
  source. Open the document and read the part you are relying on.
- Prefer the primary source: the specification over a blog post about it, the
  repository over a review of it, the paper over its press release.
- Record where the claim lives — file and line, section heading, page, or a
  quoted sentence — not just the URL. A link that resolves to a long document is
  a pointer, not a citation.

## Say what it says, then what you make of it

Keep three things visibly separate, because mixing them is how an inference
becomes a "fact":

1. **What the source states** — quote it exactly. If you cut words out of a
   quotation, mark the cut; never tighten a quote into a stronger claim.
2. **What you infer** — mark it as yours ("this implies", "I read this as").
   The inference is often the valuable part; it must not wear the source's
   authority.
3. **What you could not establish** — say so plainly. "Not found" and "the
   sources disagree" are findings. An empty section filled with a guess is not.

Numbers, dates, and direct quotations are where this matters most, and where a
paraphrase drifts fastest. Give the figure and its unit and date as the source
gives them.

## Keep the ledger honest

- Every claim tied to a citation, every citation tied to a claim. A reference
  list nobody can map back to a sentence is decoration.
- Do not pad. One strong source beats five that restate each other; if three
  pages repeat one original, cite the original.
- Check the link resolves and goes where you say. When a source has moved or
  died, cite what you saw and note the state, rather than quoting from memory.
- When a claim came from the user, from a brief, or from an earlier document in
  the project, label it as that; it is context, not independent evidence.

## Working with a running document

- Put the citation next to the sentence it supports, not only in a bibliography
  at the end; the reader should not have to hunt.
- When a source is later contradicted, correct the sentence and the citation
  together. A stale footnote under a revised claim is worse than none.
- If the document is meant to be reviewed, keep the raw material — fetched
  pages, quoted excerpts, exact commands and their output — so a reviewer can
  re-derive a finding without redoing the search.
- Flag load-bearing claims that rest on a single source. That is where an error
  propagates furthest.

## Where this goes wrong

- Citing from memory, which produces plausible titles and invented page numbers.
- A paraphrase in quotation marks.
- A confidence the sources do not support, especially about numbers.
- Omitting the counterexample: the one search result that contradicts the clean
  story is the most important one to report.
