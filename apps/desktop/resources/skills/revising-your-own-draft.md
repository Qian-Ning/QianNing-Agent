# Revising your own draft

The first draft's job was to exist. The revision's job is to make it say what you
meant to the person who will read it, who does not have your context and cannot
hear your intent.

## Revise in passes, one job each

Doing everything at once means doing none of it well, and it means rewriting
sentences you are about to delete.

1. **Structure**: what is the argument, what order should the parts be in, what is
   missing, what repeats. Cut at this level. It is far cheaper here than at any
   later pass.
2. **Paragraph**: does each one make a single point, does it start with the point,
   does the next paragraph follow.
3. **Sentence**: rhythm, length, clarity. Only now.
4. **Line**: typos, punctuation, consistency, names and numbers.

**Never polish a sentence before you know the section survives.** The most
expensive mistake in revision is careful work on text that gets cut, and it feels
exactly like progress.

## Get distance before you trust your reading

You are the worst reader of your own draft, because you read what you meant
rather than what is there.

- **Read it aloud.** Your ear catches what your eye corrects for free.
- **Change the format**: print it, change the font, read it on a phone. The
  unfamiliar rendering makes the text visible again.
- **Leave it a day** between writing and revising, whenever the deadline allows.
  Time is distance, and distance is the whole point.
- **Read it backwards**, paragraph by paragraph, when you are looking only for
  proof and consistency.

## Cut first, and cut your own tells

- The first paragraph is usually the second draft's second paragraph: it exists to
  get you started. Delete it and see whether the piece opens better.
- **Every sentence earns its place**, or it goes. The test is not "is this true"
  but "does the reader need it, here".
- Learn your own tics and search for them. Every writer has a list: the em-dash
  habit, the qualifying clause at the end, `just`, `simply`, `kind of`, `very`,
  a sentence that restates the previous one in more words. A list of your own is
  more useful than a general list of bad words.
- Cut hedges that carry no information. Where the uncertainty matters, keep it and
  name the reason; where it does not, it is noise.

## The reader cannot hear your intent

- If a reader misreads something, it is written wrong. "They did not get it" is
  never the finding.
- Assume no context beyond what the page carries. The thing you were thinking
  about when you wrote the sentence is not on the page.
- Name the reader's next question and answer it before they ask. Where they will
  disagree, say so, and meet the objection rather than avoiding it.
- Read once as a hostile reader: find the weakest claim and try to break it. If
  you can, the draft should have said so first.

## The traps specific to your own work

- **Defending instead of fixing.** When you notice something wrong, the impulse is
  to explain it to yourself rather than change the text. The reader will not be
  there for the explanation.
- **Confusing familiarity with clarity.** A sentence you have read twenty times
  feels clear. Give it to someone who has not.
- **Revising forever.** Revision becomes avoidance at the point where the changes
  stop being improvements: no more cuts, only different words. That is the signal
  to stop.
- **Rewriting from scratch too early.** Sometimes the structure is wrong and a
  new draft is right. Often the structure is fine and the draft needs the passes
  above. Tell the two apart before spending a day on the wrong one.

## Finishing

- Decide what "done" means before you start revising, or it will be defined for
  you by exhaustion.
- Read the final version once more for the thing revisions break most: transitions
  that no longer connect, a term renamed in one place and not another, an example
  left over from a cut section.
- Check the boring things: names spelled consistently, numbers that match the
  source, links that resolve, code samples that run. A reader who finds one of
  these stops trusting the whole piece.
- Then stop. A draft whose remaining changes are lateral is finished.
