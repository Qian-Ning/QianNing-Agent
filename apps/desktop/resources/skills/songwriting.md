# Writing a song that can be sung

Lyrics are the only kind of writing where the page is a draft and the melody is
the editor. A line that reads beautifully and has one syllable too many will be
mangled in performance — the singer drops a word, or crams two into one beat, and
the meaning goes with the syllable that got cut. So the unit of work is not the
line, it is the line against a bar.

## Structure is an economy, not a template

The verse spends; the chorus collects. Everything else follows from that.

- **The chorus arrives by about 0:45** in a three-minute song. Later than that
  and the listener has decided before being asked.
- **Verse one is the shortest and least decorated.** It has to earn attention
  while the listener is still deciding. Verse two can be longer, because by then
  the chorus has bought you patience.
- **The chorus repeats without new information.** Its job is recognition. If the
  second chorus has a different line, it is a second verse wearing the tune.
- **The last chorus is the one that changes** — a key lift, a doubled vocal, an
  extra tag, the title sung twice. That is where the song's ending lives, not in
  a long fade.
- **A bridge exists to make the final chorus sound new.** Four to eight bars,
  new harmony or new perspective, then out. A bridge that restates the verse is
  a third verse with a different name, and it costs twelve seconds of a song
  that had none to spare.

Bar arithmetic keeps this honest. At 100 bpm in 4/4 a bar is 2.4 s; an eight-bar
verse is 19.2 s and a three-and-a-half-minute song is 87 bars. So: intro 4 bars,
verse 8, chorus 8, verse 8, chorus 8, bridge 4, chorus 8, tag 4 is 52 bars —
two minutes — and leaves room for a second chorus repeat or a longer intro. If
your plan does not add up to the target length in bars, it will not add up when
you record it either.

## Prosody: the melody decides which syllable is loud

Speech has stress, and melody has beats. When they agree, a line sounds
inevitable. When they disagree, the singer moves the stress to fit the tune and
the sentence means something else.

Take a line with five natural stresses —

> I **keep** think**ing** a**bout** the **way** you **left**

— and put it in a bar with four strong beats. One stress has to give. In practice
the singer swallows `about` to `'bout` so that `way` lands on the beat. The line
that gets sung is not the line that was written, and nobody decided that; it fell
out of the arithmetic.

Two ways to handle it, and only two:

- **Write the elision in.** If the tune wants one syllable and the word has two,
  write `'bout`, `'cause`, `ev'ry`. The singer will do it anyway; better that the
  page agrees.
- **Change the words so the stresses fit.** Five stresses into four beats is not
  a performance problem, it is a writing problem. Cut a stress: `I keep thinking
  how you left` is four.

Do not solve it by adding a note. A melody that grows a syllable every time a
line needs one stops having a shape, and by the third verse the tune is
unrecognisable.

### You cannot count syllables with a rule

Checked against twelve common words, a vowel-group counter with a silent-`e`
rule got **four wrong**: `every` (3, is 2 — or 3 when sung `ev-er-y`), `business`
(3, is 2), `quiet` (1, is 2), `poem` (1, is 2). It got `fire` right by accident,
because the silent-`e` rule that misfires elsewhere happens to give the right
answer there.

Which means: count by singing the line against the melody, not by counting vowel
groups. The dictionary is not the score either — `every`, `different`, and
`chocolate` each have two pronunciations, and the one that matters is the one
the tune has room for.

The practical version of the check is a table with one row per line:

| bar | beats | line | stresses on beats |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | 4 | I keep thinking how you left | 1, 2, 3, 4 |

If a row has more stresses than beats, the line is wrong before the melody is.
If the same beat carries two stresses in every verse, the tune has a hole in it.

## A line that scans, and a line that lands

Scansion is countable: syllables per bar, stresses on beats, rhyme in place. A
line can pass every count and still say nothing, because counts measure the
shape and not the content.

| scans (10 syllables, 5 stresses) | lands (10 syllables, 5 stresses) |
|---|---|
| I keep thinking about the way you left | Your keys are still cold on the kitchen tile |
| And I don't know what to do without you | The clock in the hall still runs an hour fast |

Both columns have the same syllable count, so both fit the same tune. The right
column is better for one reason: it ends on a **thing** instead of a feeling.
`Cold keys on a tile` is an image the listener completes; `the way you left` is a
label for an emotion the listener has to supply. The rule behind it:

- **End the line on the concrete noun.** The last stressed word is the one the
  melody holds and the listener remembers; put the object there, not the
  abstraction.
- **One image per line.** Two images compete and neither lands.
- **Do not explain the image in the next line.** Trust the object. `Your keys
  are still cold on the kitchen tile` does not need `and that means you're gone`.
- **Specific beats general.** `An hour fast` is a fact; `so lost` is a mood.

The same distinction applies to the chorus, where it is sharper: a chorus is
remembered as a phrase, and a phrase made of abstractions is not remembered at
all. If the hook cannot be sung by someone who has heard it twice, it is too
long or too clever.

## Revision passes

Each pass has one concern. Doing them at once means fixing the count while
breaking the image and not noticing.

**1. The sing pass.** Sing every line against the melody, badly, out loud. Mark
the syllables that fight the beat. This finds what reading cannot.

**2. The stress pass.** Write the beat number under each stressed syllable. Look
for two stresses on one beat, and for a bar with none — an empty strong beat is
usually where a word got dropped.

**3. The cut pass** — one syllable per line, every line. Candidates, in order:
articles (`the`, `a`), `that` as a conjunction, `just`, `well`, `so`, `really`,
`in order to` → `to`, `I don't know` → `don't know`, and any `-ing` that can be
the base verb. Cutting one syllable from ten lines is a tighter song; cutting
none means the lines were written to the page.

**4. The image pass.** Circle every abstraction — love, pain, lost, free, home,
alone, forever. Replace at least half with something you could photograph. Keep
the ones in the chorus: a chorus is allowed one abstraction, because it is the
thing the images in the verses point at.

**5. The worst-line pass.** There is one line you skip when you read it back.
Rewrite that one first, before the ones that are nearly good.

**6. The cold-read pass.** Read the lyric with no melody, aloud. Anything that
sounds like a greeting card or a sentence nobody would say has to go, however
well it sings.

## Rhyme

- **Rhyme the end of the thought, not the end of the line.** A rhyme that lands
  mid-clause pulls the stress onto a word that does not deserve it.
- **Do not rhyme a word with itself**, and do not rhyme `-tion` with `-tion` in
  consecutive lines; the ear hears the suffix, not the word.
- **A near-rhyme in the chorus weakens the hook.** Verses can carry slant rhyme
  all day; the chorus is the one place the ear expects the full landing.
- **One strong rhyme per quatrain is enough.** Four perfect rhymes in four lines
  is a nursery rhyme, and it makes the content feel chosen to fit the sound.
- **The last line of the chorus should rhyme with the title**, because that is
  the pair the listener will carry out of the room.

If a rhyme forces a word you would not otherwise write, the line is now about
the rhyme. Change the rhyme scheme, not the meaning — the second line of a couplet
is usually the one that can move.

## Working with the melody

- **One idea per line, one turn per verse.** A verse that argues two things is
  two verses and the second one is missing.
- **Put the highest note on the title.** That is the note people remember, and
  it should carry the phrase you want repeated.
- **Keep the range inside about an octave and a half** for anything a room is
  meant to sing. Wider is a performance, not a chorus.
- **Syllabic in the verse, room to stretch in the chorus.** A chorus line with
  one long vowel on the title gives the singer somewhere to lean; a verse with
  the same gives the song nowhere to go.
- **A pre-chorus exists to solve a specific problem**: the verse and the chorus
  are too close in energy, or the chorus arrives without lift. If neither is
  true, the pre-chorus is eight bars of the song doing nothing.

## Where this goes wrong

- Writing to the page and fixing the prosody in the vocal booth, where the fix
  is always to drop a word.
- Counting syllables with a rule, then trusting the count over the sung line.
- A chorus that restates the verse in a higher register and calls it a hook.
- Every line ending on an abstraction, so the song is technically about
  something and specifically about nothing.
- Four perfect rhymes per verse, and a lyric that exists to serve them.
- A bridge that is a third verse, then a final chorus that has nothing to
  release.
- Cutting nothing in revision. A first draft that survives unedited is a draft
  that was never read closely.
