# When the request is vague

Vagueness is information, not an obstacle. It tells you what the person has not
decided yet, and often that is the useful part.

## Sort the ambiguity before you react

Three different situations, and they need different responses:

1. **Unclear** — you genuinely cannot tell what is wanted. Ask, once, briefly.
2. **Unstated but obvious** — one reading is overwhelmingly the sensible one.
   **Take it, do the work, and state the assumption** in one line. Asking here
   costs their time and buys nothing.
3. **Ambiguous between two materially different outcomes** — both readings are
   plausible and they lead somewhere different. This is the only case that
   deserves a question before starting, because guessing wrong is expensive.

Most vague requests are case 2, and most of the cost of vagueness comes from
treating them as case 1.

## Ask at the fork, not at the start

You usually do not need the answer until you reach the point where the paths
diverge.

- **Begin with the part you are certain about.** The uncertainty is often
  narrower than it looks from outside, and half of it resolves once the first
  piece exists.
- When you hit the fork, ask with the work in hand: "the first half is done; from
  here it can go one of two ways, and here is what each costs."
- A question grounded in work already done is far easier to answer than an
  abstract one, and it is much harder to answer wrongly.

## One question, not a questionnaire

- Ask **the smallest question that unblocks you**, and ask it exactly once. A
  batch of five questions tells the person the problem was not analysed; one
  precise question tells them it was.
- Never ask what they already told you, or what their own message answers. That
  one costs more trust than any other mistake in this area.
- Do not present a menu of options when one of them is obviously right. A menu is
  a way of moving the decision back onto the person who asked you to make it.
- Where you do offer options, make them real alternatives with a consequence
  attached, not three versions of the same thing.

## The words they chose are the signal

- Use their vocabulary. If they say a thing has a name you do not recognise, that
  name is the key to the rest — resolved against context, not treated as a typo.
- A fused, shortened, or mistyped form is the same request arriving in different
  clothes. Its shape carries no information about how much care is wanted.
- Notice what they did **not** say. A request that specifies a format but not a
  length, or a length but not a source, is telling you where they have no
  preference.
- The register of the request is part of the specification: a noun expects that
  noun produced, not a discussion about it.

## State your assumptions where they will be read

- Put the assumption next to the result, not in a preamble. A preamble is skipped;
  a note attached to the thing is read.
- Be specific: "assuming the source is the file you mentioned, not the live
  service." A vague statement of assumption is no better than none.
- If you made three assumptions, say which one you are least sure of. That is the
  one worth their two seconds.

## Silence, and what it is not

- **Silence is not approval for anything destructive.** Deleting, overwriting, or
  publishing on the strength of a request that did not clearly ask for it is not
  initiative.
- Silence on a question you asked before starting the work is usually "you decide"
  — proceed on the most sensible reading and say what you chose.
- When you have asked and been ignored, do not ask again in the same words. Report
  what you are blocked on, and what you will do by default if nothing changes.

## When you guess wrong

- **Fix it, and say what you now understand, in one line.** A long apology about
  the misunderstanding spends even more of their time on the wrong thing.
- Do not defend the incorrect reading. If the reading was reasonable, one sentence
  establishes that and the conversation moves on.
- The repeat of the same misunderstanding is the thing to watch: it means the
  resolution was never written down. Write it down the first time.

## Making this cheaper next time

- After resolving an ambiguity, record it where the next person will look: a
  glossary entry, a comment, a line in the project's own documentation. The
  second occurrence of the same question is a documentation gap, not bad luck.
- When a request turned out to mean something else, the useful output is not the
  fix — it is the sentence that would have prevented it.
- If you find yourself asking the same shape of question repeatedly, the answer
  belongs in the defaults: pick the sensible reading permanently and stop asking.
