# Pacing and tension

## Tension is not conflict

Conflict is two people wanting incompatible things. Tension is narrower and more
useful: **a want under resistance, where the outcome is not yet known**.

- Two people who want the same thing and cannot both have it is a scene, even
  with nobody raising their voice.
- A character who wants nothing in a scene has no tension regardless of what is
  happening. Explosions are not tension; they are volume.
- The POV must want something **in this scene** — not in the story, in the scene.
  The want can be small (to leave without being seen) as long as it is specific
  and obstructed.

## "Slow" is a diagnosis, not a property

When a reader says a section is slow, the reflex is to cut words. That is usually
wrong, because the real cause is one of these:

- **Nothing is at risk in the immediate moment** — the events are large but the
  next thirty seconds cost nothing.
- **The stakes are too distant** — the reader has been told a thing matters, but
  not seen it press on anyone yet.
- **The reader does not know what to be afraid of** — they have no prediction to
  make, so they cannot anticipate, so they wait.
- **The same beat is repeating** — the third conversation about the same doubt
  needs to be the one where it breaks or is abandoned.
- **The scene is a summary wearing a scene's clothes** — events recounted rather
  than lived.

Match the fix to the cause. Cutting words out of a scene with no risk produces a
shorter scene with no risk.

## Summary and scene

- **Summarise what nobody resists; dramatise what they do.** The technique is a
  choice per beat, not a style for the whole book.
- Compress the ordinary — travel, meals, arrangements — unless the ordinary is
  where the pressure is. A quiet dinner is a scene when someone at the table is
  holding something back.
- **Enter late and leave early.** Start at the moment something changes and end
  before the aftermath settles, so the reader does the arithmetic of the
  consequence themselves.

## What the reader knows

This is where most tension is won or lost.

- **A secret the reader does not know exists is not tension; it is absence.** The
  reader must either know the danger the character does not, or know there is
  something they have not been told. Both generate anticipation; neither is
  withholding information for its own sake.
- Let the reader see the trap closing that the character cannot see. That is the
  most reliable tension available, and it costs nothing but structure.
- Withholding the fact that a character is lying is fine. Withholding that there
  is anything to lie about is not structure, it is a trick played on a reader who
  had no chance to be afraid.
- Reveal at the moment the reveal **costs** something. Information delivered for
  free changes nothing.

## Rhythm

- **Tension needs variation.** A scene that only escalates reaches a plateau, and
  a plateau makes the peak meaningless. Alternate pressure and release at the
  scale of beats, scenes, and chapters.
- Sentence length carries speed. Short sentences read fast, long ones read slow —
  and relentless short sentences become monotone, which reads as shouting.
- Dialogue accelerates a scene. Description decelerates it. Neither is better;
  both are gears.
- An interruption is the cheapest way to indicate that a scene does not belong to
  the person speaking.

## Clocks, deadlines, and endings

- A clock is only as good as the cost of missing it. "The train leaves in an hour"
  is tension only if being on it matters to someone in the scene.
- **End a scene or a chapter on a turn, not a resolution.** A resolution is a place
  the reader can put the book down; a turn is not. That is the whole job of a
  chapter break.
- The turn does not have to be a cliffhanger. A decision made, an admission
  avoided, a question asked and not answered — all leave the reader with
  unfinished business.
- Do not end every chapter on a turn with the same shape. Chapter breaks are not
  a template, and readers learn to skim a formula within three repetitions.

## The scene test

Three sentences, and if any is empty the scene is the problem:

1. **What does the POV want right now?**
2. **What stands in the way, specifically?**
3. **What happens if they fail — and does anyone in the scene stand to lose
   something they cannot get back?**

If the third answer is "nothing", no amount of cutting will fix the scene.

## Where to cut, when a section does have to get shorter

- The parts you would skim as a reader. Trust that instinct; it is the same
  instinct the reader will have.
- Repetition of a beat already made — keep the strongest instance, cut its twins.
- Arrivals, departures, and the mechanics of getting from one place to another,
  unless the journey is the scene.
- Anything the reader already understands, explained a second time for the
  author's comfort.
