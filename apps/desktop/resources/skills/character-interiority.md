# Interiority: the character's inner life on the page

A flat scene is usually not missing action. It is missing a mind. The reader is
inside someone's head for the whole book, and what that head does with what
happens is the difference between watching and living.

## Do not report feelings; render them

"She was furious" is a report. The reader accepts it and feels nothing.

- **The body arrives before the name.** The set-down cup, the too-even voice, the
  breath held half a beat too long, the hand that goes to something to hold.
- Name the feeling only after it has been earned, if at all. Naming it first
  pre-empts the reader's own recognition, which is the whole pleasure.
- Small physical detail beats large declared emotion. The bigger the feeling, the
  less the character is able to say about it — which is exactly the point.

## Use the narration's voice, not a report from outside

The strongest tool in this area is letting the narration carry the character's
judgment without announcing it.

- Not "she thought the room was ugly", but "the room had been decorated by
  someone with money and no eye".
- No "he felt that" or "she realised that" in front of everything. The filtering
  words put distance between the reader and the thought.
- The narration's diction, rhythm, and vocabulary become the character's in that
  scene. A precise person narrates precisely; a person in shock narrates in short
  flat sentences.
- Keep it per-scene and consistent. Interiority that flickers between voices reads
  as the author, not the character.

## What they notice is who they are

Characterisation lives in attention, and so does pacing.

- A locksmith counts locks; a liar watches hands; someone who grew up poor prices
  the room on entering it. What the POV notices is free, continuous
  characterisation.
- What they **never** notice is equally revealing, and the reader will pick it up
  even if the character does not.
- The detail chosen by the character is characterisation; the detail chosen by the
  author is research showing through. That distinction is most of the craft here.

## The unsaid is where the depth is

- **What the character refuses to think about** is more informative than what they
  dwell on. In a scene, ask: what is this person steering away from?
- Let them almost arrive at the thought and turn aside. The gap is the reader's
  work, and the reader is better at it than you are.
- Contradiction: people want incompatible things and act as though they can have
  both. **A person who wants two things that cannot both be true is the
  interesting case**, and interiority is where the reader sees both pulls.

## Let the past press on the present

- Do not stop for a flashback to explain why the present hurts. The past should
  bend the present: what they notice, what they avoid, how they respond to a thing
  that reminds them.
- One remembered detail, placed where it lands, is stronger than a scene of
  backstory. Give the reader the detail they can do the arithmetic with.
- If you need the flashback, ask whether the story is actually about the past. If
  it is, the past is the present scene.

## Do not let the character diagnose themselves

- "I was afraid because my father left" is a person conducting their own therapy
  in a scene that should be about something else. Insight arrives slowly, in the
  wrong words, or not at all.
- Characters are wrong about themselves, and the reader can see it. Give the
  reader the evidence and let them arrive first — the gap between what a character
  believes about themselves and what the page shows is the most durable kind of
  characterisation there is.

## Restraint is a technique, not a virtue

- The largest feeling is often rendered smallest: a flat sentence after the blow,
  a mundane observation in the worst moment. This is how shock actually reads.
- The refusal to name the emotion can be overdone too — pages of withheld feeling
  read as opacity. Somewhere, the reader has to be let in, even if it is one line.
- The test for a scene: **does something happen inside the POV character?** If the
  events are large and the mind is unchanged, the scene is a description of
  events, not a scene.

## Per-scene checklist

- What does this character want, right now, in this scene?
- What are they refusing to think about?
- What does their body do that the mouth does not?
- What do they notice that only they would notice?
- If the narration had to describe the room in this person's words, what would
  those words be?
- Where is the reader let in, even briefly?
